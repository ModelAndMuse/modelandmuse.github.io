While The Ceiling MovesWhile The Ceiling Moves

Amber & Ink4:08~131 BPM16:9Times from the word timings

A panic attack seen from the floor of one kitchen at night, and the person who stays. The room is the main character. It tips and comes apart as the song builds, then settles until, by the outro, it is completely still.

The rules of the film

  • We are the one on the floor

    The camera is the narrator's eyes. No faces, ever. The other person is a presence: warm light, a line-drawn hand, a voice.

  • Abstract, with flashes of the real

    The low-poly room is the disconnected state. Photoreal flashes cut in like sensory overload. In the final chorus realism comes back and stays, and being real becomes the comfort.

  • Two voices, two colours

    The narrator's words are cold fluorescent white. Everything the other person says is warm amber. The light in the room shifts from one to the other over the film.

  • Stillness is the ending

    One designed curve drives every wobble, tilt and echo. It peaks as the floor gives way, then falls to zero. The last shots are the first shots, held still.

The arc

Unease: drives every distortion parameterRealism: brief flashes, then held

Visual systems

  • The room

    One low-poly kitchen built in Blender, rendered in Eevee. It tips, breathes and comes apart; the camera itself stays level.

    Unease curve, drums, bass

  • The tile grid

    Floor, wall and ceiling tiles as instanced geometry. Every banjo note lights a tile: pitch picks the column, the onset fires it.

    Banjo (guitar stem) MIDI, re-timed to audio

  • Doubling

    Everything comes in twice as loud: outlines and whole frames are rendered twice with an offset that spikes on hits and shrinks to zero by the end.

    Snare, unease curve

  • Line hands

    Hands drawn as thin line art (Grease Pencil or SVG). The outline drifts off the hand while dissociated and sits exactly on it at the end.

    Lead vocal envelope

  • Lyrics

    Every line on screen, never as plain subtitles: woven into the room when calm, a storm of copies and fragments when the unease is high. Rules below.

    Word timings, unease curve

  • Breath

    The bridge's box breathing drawn as a square, one side per bar, in time with the music.

    Tempo map

  • Realism

    Photoreal inserts: 2-6 frame flashes early on, then held, warm hands in the final chorus. Codex stills or Cycles renders for now; your own footage drops into the same slots if you get some.

    Kick and snare hits; final chorus

How the lyrics behave

  • Always readable

    Every word appears clean and legible at the moment it is sung. No exceptions.

  • The storm surrounds it

    The unease curve decides how much chaos surrounds that clean word: extra copies, fragments, wrong scales and angles, 1-2 frame flashes, earlier lines resurfacing.

  • The other voice never storms

    The other person's words (warm amber) are always clean and still. 'I'm here' is the one steady thing in the frame.

  • Instrumentals carry fragments

    With nothing sung, only broken pieces of words drift through, deliberately unreadable.

How it gets built

  1. Analyse

    Beat grid from the MIDI tempo map, calibrated to the audio. Per-stem envelopes and onsets at the video frame rate. Word timings from Whisper.

  2. Build scenes

    Blender scenes read the analysis data and keyframe themselves; the same kitchen set serves most shots.

  3. 2D layers

    Type, line hands and the breathing square drawn from code (SVG/Cairo or Grease Pencil) on the same timeline.

  4. Edit

    A Python edit decision list cuts and composites the layers with ffmpeg, so every cut lands on a real beat.

Decided

  • 16:9, and the kitchen stays: the lyrics name it (the sink, the tiles, the kitchen itself, the door).
  • The world is abstract and low-poly; realism appears only as flashes and as the held hands.
  • The closing wide shot keeps its faceless low-poly figures, with the hands moments just before it.
  • Every lyric is on screen, woven into the room or scattered as a storm, never as plain subtitles.
  • Realism comes from Codex stills or Cycles renders for now; your footage replaces them if it arrives.
